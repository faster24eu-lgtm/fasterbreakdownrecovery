How to Start a Breakdown Recovery Business

Most recovery firms start with one person, one truck and a phone. Here's what you actually need to get going, where the work comes from, and the mistakes that cost new operators the most, based on what recovery people say once they've been through it.

Start small: one truck, the work you can actually do

Very few recovery businesses start with a fleet. A common route is a car trailer behind a 4x4 or a 3.5-tonne tilt-and-slide or beavertail, moving cars for friends, local garages and the odd breakdown, then adding vehicles as the work comes in. Starting small keeps your costs low while you find out whether the work in your area pays.

A trailer is cheap, but reversing it down one-way streets and tight residential roads gets old quickly. A recovery truck lets you load, drive and drop off without the faff, which is why most people move to one as soon as they can.

Choosing your first truck: watch the payload

On a 3.5-tonne recovery truck, what you can legally carry is the gross weight minus the truck itself, and the bed is a big part of that. A heavy steel body can leave you with too little payload to carry an average car legally. Before you buy, get the truck weighed on a public weighbridge and work out the real payload. An aluminium body is lighter and leaves more room for the vehicle on the back.

  • Winch: rated for the vehicles you'll actually move, not the smallest one. A winch that gives up halfway through loading a heavy van is a lesson many people learn the hard way.
  • Size of job: large vans, motorhomes and anything over about 3.5 tonnes need a bigger vehicle, a bigger licence and often different insurance. Say no, or pass the job on (see below), rather than overload.
  • Backup: once you depend on the income, a broken-down recovery truck is a day without work. Many operators keep an older second vehicle as a spare.

Licence: what you can drive

A standard category B car licence covers vehicles up to 3,500 kg maximum authorised mass. Between 3.5 and 7.5 tonnes you need category C1, and above that category C (often called Class 2). If you passed your car test before 1 January 1997 you may already have C1. A bigger licence opens up second-hand 7.5-tonne and larger trucks, which can cost less than a new 3.5-tonne truck.

Recovery vehicles have their own rules and exemptions for operator licensing, tachographs and vehicle tax, and those rules come with conditions. Check the current guidance from the DVSA on GOV.UK for the vehicle you plan to buy before you buy it.

Insurance: the expensive bit you can’t skip

Insurance is usually the biggest shock for new operators, and it's the part you can't cut corners on. A private or panel-van policy on a recovery truck is not enough: if something goes wrong, you're not covered. Speak to a broker who knows recovery and expect to need:

  • Motor trade or recovery cover that names what you actually do: breakdown recovery, transport, accident recovery, motorway work, mobile tyres.
  • Public liability, for damage or injury you cause while working.
  • Goods in transit, for the customer's vehicle while it's on your truck.
  • Employers' liability as soon as you take on staff. In the UK this is a legal requirement for most employers.

Having the right paperwork pays off: garages, fleets and contractors will ask for proof of cover before they give you work.

PAS 43 and safe working

PAS 43 is the British standard for safe working in vehicle breakdown, recovery and removal. It isn't a legal requirement, and some independent operators run for years without it. Many contractors and larger customers do ask for it, though. Even if you don't certify, work to the same habits: daily vehicle and equipment checks written down, cones, beacons, high-vis and the right straps and snatch blocks on board, and a clear rule that nobody works in live lanes.

Where the work comes from

  • Local garages: the steady base for most small firms. Turn up, be reliable, be on time and they'll keep calling you.
  • The public: a simple website, a Google Business Profile, leaflets and business cards. Breakdowns are local and urgent, so being easy to find nearby matters more than anything.
  • Trade and dealers: moving bought and sold cars, auction collections, garage-to-garage transport.
  • Councils and contractors: abandoned vehicles, contract work. These come with paperwork, keyless and locked vehicles, and payment terms.

Motor clubs and contract work: do the maths

Contract work fills the diary, but check the numbers before you sign. Long runs for low fixed rates, 30 to 60 days to get paid, and staff on an hourly wage can leave you busy all day and earning very little. Plenty of operators end up as what one calls “busy fools”. Work out what a job really costs you in fuel, wages, wear and time before agreeing a rate, and don't be afraid to drop contracts that don't pay on time.

Work smarter: pass on what you can’t do

You don't have to own a vehicle for every job. Build a list of trusted operators nearby and further afield, with what each of them can lift. When a job is too big, too far or comes in when you're busy, pass it on at a fair price and keep a small admin fee for taking the enquiry. The customer gets help faster, you keep the customer, and the favour usually comes back. Driving four hours for one job can cost you the local work you'd have done in that time.

Growing: staff, premises and what they really cost

More trucks and staff mean more work, but also wages every week whether the phone rings or not, more insurance claims (a scraped bumper by a driver still goes on your record), more repairs and, often, a yard or unit with rent and business rates. Some operators find they earned more as a one-man band. Grow because the work is there and the numbers work, not just because you can.

Look after your trucks and yourself

Recovery trucks are a target for thieves. Use trackers, steering and wheel locks and immobilisers, park where cameras can see, and keep both keys safe: your insurer will ask. Working from home can also bring complaints from neighbours or the council about heavy vehicles on the street, so think about where the trucks will live before you buy them.

And plan for the hours. Recovery is 24/7, and plenty of people burn out in the first few years. Decide how big you want to be and protect some time off.

Starting a Recovery Business: FAQs

How much does it cost to start a breakdown recovery business?

The biggest costs are the truck, insurance and equipment. A used trailer and 4x4 is the cheapest start; a used 3.5-tonne recovery truck costs more but is far more practical. Get insurance quotes before you buy anything: for many new operators the premium is a bigger monthly cost than the truck.

Can I drive a recovery truck on a car licence?

On a category B licence you can drive vehicles up to 3,500 kg maximum authorised mass, which covers most 3.5-tonne recovery trucks. Heavier trucks need C1 (up to 7.5 tonnes) or C. Remember that the car on the back has to fit within the truck's payload.

Do I need PAS 43 to start?

No, it isn't a legal requirement. Many contractors and larger customers ask for it, so it can open doors to that work. Whether it's worth the cost depends on where your work comes from.

What insurance does a recovery business need?

Typically a motor trade or recovery policy that covers the work you do, public liability and goods in transit, plus employers' liability once you have staff. Use a broker who knows recovery and make sure every activity you do is declared.

Should I work for the motor clubs?

It can give you volume when you start, but check the rates, the distances and the payment terms carefully. Many operators find that garages, trade customers and the public pay better.

What should I do with jobs I can't handle?

Pass them to a trusted operator who can, at a fair price, and keep the customer. A list of reliable firms and what they can lift is one of the most useful things a small recovery business can have.
